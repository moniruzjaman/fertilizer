01 — Executive Summary

Bangladesh's agricultural sector confronts a paradox: over 1.365 million tonnes of fertilizer sit in government warehouses, yet farmers in Rajshahi, Jashore, Kurigram, and Lalmonirhat face acute shortages, paying 50–100% above official prices. The crisis is not one of supply, but of distribution integrity, institutional accountability, and subsidy targeting.

This report diagnoses the root causes of systemic failure; inventories the digital and institutional assets already at our disposal; distills actionable lessons from India, Vietnam, China, and the Philippines; and presents a phased, technically feasible, and politically urgent reform roadmap. It aligns the reform agenda with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), FAO strategic priorities, and agricultural diplomacy, while grounding the vision in the nationalist, farmer-first ethos of Bir Uttam President Ziaur Rahman—who envisioned a self-reliant Bangladesh where the dignity of the Krishak is non-negotiable and institutional discipline is the bedrock of governance.

The core recommendation: Transition from a blanket, dealer-centric subsidy model to a biometrically authenticated, land-linked, digitally tracked distribution system that ensures the right farmer receives the right input, at the right price, at the right time.

02 — The Current Crisis: Root Cause Analysis

2.1 The Distribution-Availability Paradox

Official data confirms sufficient national stocks and approved imports of 365,000 tonnes at a cost of Tk 2,562 crore. Yet field-level reality diverges sharply:

District / Division Official Price Market Price Allocation vs. Lifted
Rajshahi (TSP)Tk 1,350 / 50kgTk 1,700–1,800—
Jashore (TSP)~Tk 26 / kgTk 52 / kg—
Gazipur (Urea)——800 / 1,876 t (43%)
Dhaka Division (8 districts)——8,965 / 19,918 t (45%)
Mymensingh Division——9,992 / 31,425 t (32%)

Root Cause: The failure is not procurement but last-mile distribution. Stocks remain in warehouses while dealers, through negligence or collusion, fail to lift allocations, creating artificial scarcity that feeds the black market.

2.2 Structural Dysfunctions

  • Collusion and Diversion: The Additional Secretary for Fertiliser Management has acknowledged that "negligence and collusion among some dealers and field-level officials" divert subsidized fertilizer to unauthorized markets.
  • Regulatory Erosion: The 2009 dealer framework has become obsolete, with sub-dealers and retailers operating with minimal accountability.
  • Information Asymmetry: Farmers lack credible, site-specific advice and rely on dealers with a vested interest in over-selling.

2.3 The Subsidy Regressivity Trap

Bangladesh's fiscal commitment is immense—Tk 17,001 crore proposed for FY2026–27, with an additional Tk 1,350 crore necessitated by global shocks. Yet the World Bank assesses that:

  • ~80% of public agricultural spending is absorbed by fertilizer subsidies.
  • The wealthiest 20% of landowners capture ~50% of subsidies.
  • The bottom 40% receive only ~15%.
  • Only ~5% of farmers apply balanced nutrients at recommended levels.

Conclusion: The current system is fiscally unsustainable, environmentally harmful, and socially regressive.

03 — Resources Already in Possession

Bangladesh is not starting from zero. Four foundational assets provide the architecture for reform:

3.1 Digital Land Mapping (BDS / EDLMS)

The Bangladesh Digital Survey and Establishment of Digital Land Management System have integrated over 20,000 mouza maps, with 138,000 more under digitization. A Memorandum of Understanding with the Republic of Korea (September 2025) is accelerating progress. This provides the geospatial data layer to link fertilizer entitlements to verified land parcels.

3.2 The Krishak Card

Launched in April 2026, the Krishak Card digitally stores approximately 120 data points per farmer. Already, 20,671 landless, marginal, and small farmers in 10 upazilas are receiving Tk 2,500 annually via mobile financial services (MFS), with planned debit-card functionality. This is the digital identity and payment layer.

3.3 The Comprehensive Policy on Fertilizer Dealer Appointment and Distribution, 2026

The new policy appoints one additional dealer per union, eliminates sub-dealers and retailers, and replaces the 2009 framework. This is the regulatory layer—provided enforcement is digitized and depoliticized.

3.4 National ID and Mobile Infrastructure

The National ID (NID) database and near-ubiquitous mobile network coverage provide the authentication and communication backbone necessary for real-time transaction verification.

04 — Regional Comparative Analysis: Lessons from Neighbors

4.1 India: The DBT Biometric Revolution

Since 2018, India's Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) in Fertilizers has operated on a "no denial" basis:

  • Mechanism: Aadhaar-enabled Point of Sale (PoS) devices authenticate farmers at retail shops; subsidies are released to companies only after verified sales.
  • Scale: 2.87 lakh retailers serve 8.4 crore farmers.
  • Evolution: India is piloting land and crop-based limits, where farmers register land and crops via app to pre-book fertilizer based on scientific need.

Lesson for Bangladesh: Biometric authentication at the point of sale is non-negotiable for eliminating diversion. The shift from blanket to land-linked quotas is the next frontier.

4.2 Vietnam: Precision and Sustainability

Vietnam's "One Must, Five Reductions" technique mandates certified seeds while reducing seeding rates, fertilizers, pesticides, water, and post-harvest losses.

  • Results: The Mekong Delta's 1 Million Hectare High-Quality, Low-Emission Rice Program achieved a 31.3% average reduction in fertilizer use and 20% reduction in nitrogen fertilizer and irrigation water.

Lesson for Bangladesh: Reducing chemical dependency through precision agriculture and organic integration improves both farmer economics and environmental resilience.

4.3 China: Zero Growth and Digital Agriculture

China's "Action Plan for Zero Growth in Chemical Fertilizer Use" (2015–2020) targeted a fundamental pivot:

  • Targets: Fertilizer utilization rates >40%; soil testing coverage >90%.
  • Digital Backbone: Over 99% of administrative villages have fiber-optic and 4G coverage; ~80% have farmer information societies.
  • Impact: Internet-informed farmers were 3.5% more fertilizer-efficient than non-users. E-commerce operators reduced chemical use to protect product reputation.

Lesson for Bangladesh: Digital infrastructure must precede or accompany subsidy reform. Market-based incentives (premium prices for sustainable produce) can drive behavior change more effectively than input subsidies alone.

4.4 The Philippines: Cooperatives and Climate-Smart Models

The Philippines demonstrates the value of farmer cooperatives (e.g., AMIAnan Farmers Entrepreneur Agriculture Cooperative) in aggregating demand and delivering extension services. However, it also warns of the dangers of inefficient subsidy reach and low nutrient use efficiency (NUE).

Lesson for Bangladesh: Cooperatives must be capacitated as intermediaries, but without digital tracking, subsidy schemes remain prone to leakage.

05 — Opportunities for Transformation

Bangladesh has a narrow window to leapfrog the worst phases of subsidy dependence experienced by its neighbors:

  1. From Blanket to Precision Subsidies: Use digital land data and soil health testing to calculate farmer-specific entitlements, eliminating capture by wealthy, large landowners.
  2. From Dealer Anarchy to Dealer Accountability: The 2026 Dealer Policy, enforced through biometric sales data, can transform dealers into service providers rather than gatekeepers of scarcity.
  3. From Import Dependence to Domestic Resilience: With >70% of demand met through imports, reviving domestic plant capacity reduces exposure to global shocks.
  4. From Subsidy to Market Incentives: Linking farmers to premium domestic and export markets that reward sustainable production can create self-sustaining efficiency gains.
  5. From Crisis Management to Predictive Governance: Satellite imagery (NDVI) and soil health databases enable demand forecasting and early warning.

06 — Technical Requirements

A reformed system requires a four-layer digital and physical architecture:

Layer Component Specification
Identity & Land Krishak Card + NID + BDS/EDLMS Biometric linkage to verified land parcels and crop declarations
Point of Sale Biometric PoS Devices Aadhaar-like authentication at dealer shops; offline capability with local server sync
Tracking & Transparency Central Digital Ledger + Blockchain Immutable record from import/production → dealer → farmer; public dashboard
Advisory & Verification National Digital Agriculture Platform + Satellite NDVI Mobile-first soil health recommendations; satellite verification of crop declarations

6.1 Hybrid Edge-Cloud Architecture

Given connectivity gaps in rural Bangladesh, local servers at the union/upazila level must process transactions offline, syncing with central servers when connectivity resumes. This ensures inclusion of remote and climate-vulnerable regions.

6.2 Soil Testing and Formula Fertilization (STFFT)

A nationwide Soil Health Card program—mirroring China's STFFT—must provide localized, crop-specific recommendations. This shifts farmer behavior from "more is better" to "precision is profitable."

08 — Cost-Benefit Analysis

8.1 Current Fiscal Burden (Status Quo)

  • FY2026–27 Allocation: Tk 17,001 crore (agricultural subsidies, predominantly fertilizer).
  • Emergency Supplement: Tk 1,350 crore (2025–26, due to global conflict).
  • Implicit Cost: Environmental degradation; farmer distress; erosion of trust in public institutions.

8.2 Projected Benefits of Reform

Indicator Current State Post-Reform Target (5 Years) Fiscal / Impact Estimate
Subsidy leakage / diversion High <5% Savings of Tk 2,000–3,000 crore annually
Share of subsidies to bottom 40% ~15% >40% Progressive reallocation
NUE (Nitrogen Use Efficiency) Low (~5% balanced application) +15–20% efficiency gain Reduced import demand; environmental savings
Dealer compliance / stock lifting 32–45% in key divisions >90% Elimination of artificial shortages
Farmer transaction time Hours/days in queues <10 minutes Productivity and dignity gains

8.3 Investment Requirements

  • Biometric PoS & Local Servers: Modest capital outlay (scalable via public-private partnership).
  • Soil Testing Expansion: Leverage existing SRDI infrastructure with incremental funding.
  • Satellite & Platform Development: Can be integrated with ongoing digital land mapping (BDS/EDLMS) and Korea-assisted projects.
  • Net Position: Reform pays for itself through leakage reduction and efficiency gains within 2–3 fiscal cycles.

09 — Preparatory Phases: A Four-Stage Roadmap

Phase I: Foundation (Months 1–12)

Enact implementing rules for the 2026 Dealer Policy with digital compliance mandates. Pilot biometric PoS in 2 divisions. Integrate Krishak Card with NID and initial BDS land parcels. Launch Soil Health Card sampling in pilot upazilas. Establish local server nodes in pilot unions.

Phase II: Integration (Months 13–24)

Nationwide rollout of biometric PoS and e-bill system (modeled on India). Link fertilizer quotas to verified land area and crop type. Deploy mobile app for crop declaration and pre-booking. Initiate satellite NDVI verification for Boro season. Activate public dashboard for transparency.

Phase III: Optimization (Months 25–36)

Transition from company-subsidy to farmer e-voucher pilots for select districts. Scale Soil Health Card to national coverage. Introduce AI/ML demand forecasting. Strengthen domestic fertilizer production to reduce import share from 70% toward 50%.

Phase IV: Sustainability (Months 37–60)

Full phased transition from blanket subsidies to targeted, land-linked entitlements. Integrate market linkage platforms rewarding sustainable practices. Establish independent oversight body. Align fully with SDG 2.4 and FAO's Better Production agenda.

11 — Alignment with SDG, FAO & Agriculture Diplomacy

11.1 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

  • SDG 1 (No Poverty): Targeted subsidies and e-vouchers directly increase disposable income of marginal farmers.
  • SDG 2 (Zero Hunger): Precision fertilization improves yield stability and soil health for long-term food security.
  • SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption & Production): STFFT and reduced chemical overuse align with sustainable resource management.
  • SDG 13 (Climate Action): Lower nitrogen runoff and greenhouse gas emissions from efficient fertilizer use.

11.2 FAO Strategic Objectives

The reform directly supports FAO's "Four Betters"—Better Production, Better Nutrition, Better Environment, Better Life—by transforming input distribution from a source of rent-seeking into a driver of sustainable intensification.

11.3 Agriculture Diplomacy

  • Bilateral Knowledge Exchange: Deepen cooperation with India on DBT-PoS logistics; with Vietnam on precision rice farming; with China on digital rural infrastructure; and with South Korea on digital land management.
  • Regional Food Security: A transparent Bangladeshi system positions the country as a leader in South Asian agricultural governance, enhancing credibility in SAARC and BIMSTEC forums.
  • Climate Diplomacy: By reducing nitrogen waste and adopting satellite-verified sustainable practices, Bangladesh strengthens its negotiating position for climate adaptation finance and NDC implementation.

12 — Walking the Path of Bir Uttam President Ziaur Rahman

Bir Uttam President Ziaur Rahman, the Father of Bangladeshi Nationalism, did not merely seek political sovereignty; he sought economic dignity for the farmer—the Krishak—whom he recognized as the backbone of the nation. His vision was one of a disciplined, self-reliant Bangladesh where rural prosperity was not a matter of charity, but of institutional justice and administrative integrity.

To walk his path today is to acknowledge that a warehouse full of fertilizer means nothing if the farmer's hands remain empty. It is to declare that the syndicate, the colluding dealer, and the negligent official are enemies of the state no less than any external threat. It is to insist that Bangladesh's sovereignty is incomplete while its food system remains captive to black markets and global price shocks.

This reform agenda honors that legacy through three nationalist imperatives:

  1. Discipline in Governance: The biometric PoS, the blockchain ledger, and the public dashboard are not merely technologies—they are instruments of institutional discipline. They ensure that the officer, the dealer, and the minister are all accountable to the farmer.
  2. Empowerment, Not Dependency: Shifting from blanket subsidies to soil-tested, land-linked entitlements treats the farmer not as a passive recipient of state charity, but as a rational economic actor capable of stewarding the land with precision and pride.
  3. Self-Reliance: By reviving domestic production, reducing import dependence, and leveraging Bangladesh's own digital infrastructure (BDS, Krishak Card, NID), we build a system that is resilient, sovereign, and future-proof.

As President Ziaur Rahman trusted the courage and capacity of the Bangladeshi people to forge their destiny, so too must this Ministry trust the Bangladeshi farmer with the tools of modernity—digital identity, scientific knowledge, and fair markets—and then get out of his way.

13 — Conclusion & Recommendations

The current fertilizer crisis is a distribution and governance crisis, not an act of nature. The resources to solve it are already within our grasp. The examples of our neighbors illuminate the path. What remains is the political will to act decisively.

Immediate Recommendations to the Honorable Minister

  1. Direct the Secretary of Agriculture to issue a circular mandating biometric PoS piloting in all divisions within 90 days, using Krishak Card and NID authentication.
  2. Instruct BADC and DAE to integrate stock allocation data with the BDS/EDLMS land parcel database, ensuring quotas are land-linked by the upcoming Boro season.
  3. Empower the Agriculture Secretary to enforce the 2026 Dealer Policy with automatic suspension (not merely warning) for dealers failing to lift stocks or selling above MRP.
  4. Request Finance Division to approve a pilot e-voucher scheme in 2 districts, transitioning subsidy delivery from companies to farmers via MFS rails.
  5. Establish an Independent Oversight Committee comprising farmer leaders, civil society, and technical experts to publish quarterly transparency reports.
  6. Task the Soil Resource Development Institute with delivering 1 million Soil Health Cards within 18 months, starting with the most fertilizer-intensive rice belts.

Bangladesh stands at a crossroads. We can continue the cycle of crisis, subsidy, and black-market profiteering.
Or we can choose the path of transparency, accountability, and farmer dignity—
the path of a truly sovereign Bangladesh.

The farmer is waiting. The technology is ready. The nation is watching.
