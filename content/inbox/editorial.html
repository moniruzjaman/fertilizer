A Record of Strength Worth Building On

Bangladesh’s fertiliser story is, first and foremost, a success story — and it deserves to be told that way. Government figures show national stocks substantially above projected requirements through the coming months, with 1.365 million tonnes already resting in government warehouses and a further 365,000 tonnes of approved imports moving through the pipeline. On any measure, the country’s procurement machinery has proven it can secure fertiliser at scale, season after season, against the pressures of international prices, shipping logistics and foreign-exchange conditions.

Behind these numbers stands something larger: a subsidy commitment of roughly Tk 17,001 crore every year. Few national budgets make so direct and so generous a promise to agriculture. It signals that fertiliser security is treated not as a commodity problem but as a food-security covenant between the state and its farmers.

And yet a quiet puzzle has emerged in the field — not a crisis of supply, but an invitation to perfect the system. Farmers in some districts have stood in queues, received restricted quantities and, in places, paid more than government-fixed prices, even as warehouses stand comfortably full.

“Supply is no longer our constraint — the opportunity now lies in distribution excellence, institutional integrity and precise subsidy targeting.”— The central opportunity of this piece

The distinction is subtle but important, and Bangladesh is far from alone in facing it: national availability is not the same as farmer-level availability. A fertiliser bag in a warehouse is a national asset; in a farmer’s hands at sowing time, it becomes a harvest. The distance between the two is precisely where the next great gain in Bangladeshi agriculture lies.

The Last-Mile Opportunity: Listening to the Field

The recent signals from the field should be read the way a good physician reads symptoms — not as indictments, but as guidance. Farmers in parts of Kurigram, Rangpur, Dinajpur, Rajshahi, Bogura and Lalmonirhat experienced restricted availability through authorised channels this season. In Kurigram’s Bhurungamari, frustration ran high enough that in August, hundreds of farmers reportedly broke open a dealer warehouse — a painful moment, and an unmistakable message that the system must move as fast as farmers’ needs.

The proximate cause appears largely administrative and entirely fixable. The transition to the new fertiliser dealer policy — a reform designed to professionalise the network — has temporarily created vacancies and uncertainty in some areas, and dealer lifting from government supply points has softened in pockets while demand spiked with the crop calendar.

The farmer’s question — and the standard to aim for

“Can I get the required fertiliser, in the required quantity, at the government-fixed price, from an authorised seller, at the time my crop requires it?” — Answering this question with an unbroken “yes” in every upazila is entirely achievable, and this piece sets out how.

What is firmly established

  • National stocks are substantially above projected requirements — procurement has succeeded.
  • The dealer-policy transition temporarily thinned parts of the retail network.
  • Farmers in several districts faced rationing and above-official prices in informal channels.
  • Demand is seasonal and geographic — national averages cannot guarantee local availability.

Under examination — attributed, not proven

  • Special Branch report (via The Daily Star): alleged collusion between some dealers and retailers to create artificial shortages.
  • Bangladesh Fertilizer Association: public warnings against hoarding and inflated prices.
These remain attributed allegations, not findings — and they should not be applied to the dealer community as a whole. The constructive path is transparent, evidence-based investigation: it protects honest dealers from blanket suspicion while assuring farmers that the system is being defended on their behalf.

Seen this way, the government’s task is not one problem but one opportunity with two supportive halves: keep supply flowing to legitimate farmers without interruption, and let transparent investigation clarify whether any individuals attempted manipulation. Both strengthen the same institution — trust.

“Blanket accusations can damage honest dealers; transparent investigation can protect both the farmer and the honest majority of the trade.”— On evidence-based accountability

Reform Is Already in Motion: The 2026 Dealer Policy as a Turning Point

It is worth saying plainly: the turbulence of recent months is not a symptom of failure — it is the growing pain of modernisation. The 2026 Dealer Policy is an attempt to build a cleaner, more accountable retail network, and every serious reform passes through an adjustment period. The task now is to carry it through confidently and kindly:

Complete transitions before peak seasons. Dealer appointments and network gaps should be closed ahead of critical fertilisation windows, so no farmer ever waits on paperwork. Define responsibilities clearly — lifting, stocking, selling at official prices — with auditable records and proportionate, predictable consequences. And just as importantly, reward compliant dealers swiftly: the honest majority of the trade should experience the new policy as recognition, not suspicion.

Handled this way, the dealer-policy transition becomes what it was always meant to be: the moment Bangladesh’s fertiliser retail turned professional.

The Structural Horizon: Turning Import Dependence into Domestic Revival

Beyond the last mile lies a longer, equally winnable campaign. More than two-thirds of Bangladesh’s annual fertiliser requirement is met through imports. In FY2025–26, state-owned factories reportedly produced around 1.06 million tonnes against national demand of roughly 6.6 million tonnes, with several plants interrupted by gas shortages, ageing machinery and raw-material constraints. Far from being a cause for alarm, this gap is a map — it shows exactly where investment in plant rehabilitation, reliable gas supply and efficiency modernisation would pay the greatest national dividend.

The same logic applies to fertiliser-use efficiency — the quiet multiplier. Balanced fertilisation, soil testing, improved nutrient-use efficiency and the integration of organic and organomineral sources reduce unnecessary dependence on purchased fertiliser while maintaining, and often improving, productivity. Every kilogram saved is a kilogram the state need not import, subsidise or transport.

Learning from the Region and the World

Bangladesh is fortunate to attempt this next step with a rich body of international experience to draw upon — from its neighbours and partners across Asia to documented programmes in Africa and Latin America. The lesson is never to copy a system wholesale, but to adapt its proven core.

Seven Actions to Complete the Journey

The encouraging truth is that Bangladesh does not need to invent anything new. The technology is ready, the policy framework exists, and the regional evidence is clear. What follows is a seven-point action agenda — none of it requires new legislation, and each step unlocks the next within a single budget cycle.

This is not a cost; it is an investment with one of the best returns available in public policy.

From Stock Management to Farmer Security — The Road to Bloom

The recent season should be remembered not as a crisis, but as the moment Bangladesh saw its next frontier clearly. The nation has proven it can procure fertiliser at scale and sustain one of the world’s largest agricultural subsidy programmes. The remaining task — distribution excellence at the last mile — is narrower, well understood, and equipped with ready solutions.

Both truths can coexist, and each strengthens the other: national abundance is real, and farmer-level assurance is achievable. The test of the system is not what appears in a warehouse report; it is what happens when a farmer walks to the authorised dealer during the critical fertilisation window.

Five promises to keep at every dealer counter

  • The correct product — always in reach.
  • The required quantity — no artificial ceilings.
  • The official price — every single sale.
  • An accountable, honoured dealer network.
  • A government that can verify every bag’s journey.

Keep these five, and every national stock figure becomes a lived reality in the field.

The immediate disruptions can be resolved quickly; the larger opportunity should not be missed. With the seven actions above — biometric authentication, land-linked allocation, a confident dealer policy, e-vouchers, independent oversight, soil health cards and a transparent digital pilot — Bangladesh can move from repeatedly responding to shortages toward detecting them before they ever reach a farmer.

The bottom line

“A warehouse full of fertiliser becomes a nation full of nourishment the moment every farmer’s hands are filled. The ultimate measure of fertiliser security is the farmer who obtains the right fertiliser, at the right price, at the right place and at the right time — and Bangladesh now holds every tool needed to deliver exactly that.”
